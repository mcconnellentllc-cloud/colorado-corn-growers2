Pulling the Plow for Colorado Corn

Posted September 25, 2026 · Updated as the work continues

Growers ask us who is actually fighting for them. These are the officials who have shown up for Colorado corn — who took the meetings, moved the bills and did the unglamorous committee work that decides whether a farm program reaches this state or stops at the Kansas line. We are glad to say so publicly, and we stand behind their work.

Colorado’s Delegation

Rep. Lauren Boebert, 4th District. The 4th carries the eastern plains and most of Colorado’s corn ground, and she has treated that as the assignment it is. She brought growers to the table at the agriculture round table at the Cow Palace in Lamar and listened to the case for a permanent disaster program written into the farm bill — aid that reaches producers who take a real loss without propping up producers who do not. That is a hard argument to make in Washington and she has carried it. Colorado corn is better represented for it.

Sen. Michael Bennet. Few members of the Senate know the farm bill file the way he does. He is a longstanding member of the Senate Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition and Forestry, chairs its Subcommittee on Conservation, Climate, Forestry and Natural Resources, helped negotiate two bipartisan farm bills, and has held more farm bill listening sessions across this state than anyone could reasonably ask of a senator. He has put in the work to understand what Colorado ground actually needs, and Colorado corn has been heard in rooms it would otherwise have missed.

We do not line up on every vote — he voted against the Agricultural Act of 2026 in committee this month, and this association wants a bill moved. We say that plainly because growers deserve the whole picture, and because a working relationship that survives disagreement is worth more than one that depends on never having any.

The Colorado General Assembly

Sen. Cleave Simpson. A fourth-generation San Luis Valley farmer and rancher who runs the Rio Grande Water Conservation District for a living — which means when he talks about water, he is describing his own working life, not a briefing memo. He sits on the Senate Agriculture and Natural Resources Committee, where Colorado’s pesticide and seed bills get decided, and he carried Colorado agriculture’s case to the United States Senate Agriculture Committee when it held its farm bill field hearing in this state. Producers here are fortunate to have somebody in that seat who has actually farmed.

Beyond Colorado

Corn policy is not written in Colorado. These are offices whose decisions land squarely on our ground, and whose doors our affiliation with SWCA helps open.

Sen. John Boozman of Arkansas, Chairman of the Senate Agriculture Committee. He has spent this Congress trying to get a farm bill across a chamber that has not made it easy. He authored the Agricultural Act of 2026 and brought it out of committee on September 16 after an August attempt fell short — the kind of persistence that separates a bill that passes from a bill that gets talked about. Inside it is the piece that matters most to corn: permanent, year-round, nationwide E15, which turns ethanol demand into law instead of a favor granted one summer at a time. Corn growers in this state owe that effort real thanks.

Sen. John Hoeven of North Dakota. A consistent friend of the ethanol industry and of the growers who supply it. He cosponsored legislation to allow year-round E15 sales and end the annual waiver scramble that has made retailers skittish about carrying the blend at all. Demand certainty is worth more to a grower than most of what gets called help.

Rep. G.T. Thompson of Pennsylvania, Chairman of the House Agriculture Committee. He took on a fight most members leave alone. H.R. 9535, the Securing Agriculture’s Workforce Act of 2026, would be the first statutory reform of the H-2A program in forty years, and it removes the seasonal requirement that has locked year-round operations out of the program. That provision reaches Colorado dairies and feedyards directly — and feedyards are corn customers. Labor that keeps a feedyard staffed keeps a buyer for our crop.

Tell Them It Matters

Officials hear from growers when something goes wrong and almost never when something goes right, which is a poor way to encourage more of the second thing. If one of these offices has done something that helped your operation, call and say so. Thank them, and then ask them to finish the job — a farm bill moved, E15 made permanent, a disaster title written in rather than bolted on after the fact.

That call takes four minutes and it is the cheapest advocacy any member of this association will ever do.
