Harvest Fuel Relief: $8 Billion, and What Is Not Decided Yet

Posted October 20, 2026

USDA will put $8 billion into one-time payments to farmers for the 2026 harvest, announced on October 19 by President Trump alongside Secretary Brooke Rollins. It is real money and it is aimed squarely at what diesel has done to this year’s cost of production. It is also, as of today, a program without a payment rate or an application window.

Both of those are expected to arrive before long. Until they do, the useful thing is to separate what USDA has actually said from what is being filled in around it — and to do the one piece of preparation that costs nothing and will matter when the window opens.

What USDA Has Actually Said

The money comes from Commodity Credit Corporation funds under the CCC Charter Act, and the Farm Service Agency will administer it. Payments are one-time, for the 2026 crop year, and are described as covering part of modeled losses rather than reimbursing fuel receipts. Nobody is going to ask for your diesel tickets.

The modelling is national. USDA says the calculation draws on FSA-reported planted acres, Economic Research Service cost-of-production data, and WASDE yield and price estimates, combined into a national loss average. The program is explicitly modelled on the earlier Farmer Bridge Assistance program, which is the most useful thing in the announcement for anyone trying to guess at the shape of it.

One caveat worth carrying into November. NASS corrected its September corn data on October 23 after a processing error reached two states and the national forecast. WASDE yields are an input to this program’s formula, so read the rate announcement closely rather than assuming the arithmetic behind it is beyond question.

Two details worth knowing now. Crop insurance enrollment is not required to participate, which is a meaningful difference from several recent programs. And producers who link a Login.gov account to USDA will get a pre-filled application online rather than starting from a blank form.

What Is Not Decided

We are going to be plain about this rather than dress it up, because the gaps are the part that determines what lands in your account.

  • No payment rates. Rates for Title I commodities — corn among them — are expected in November.
  • No application window. No open or close date has been announced.
  • No method yet for crops outside Title I. USDA says that will come later.
  • No per-crop formula. The inputs have been named; how they combine into a rate for corn has not.

Anyone quoting you a per-acre figure for this program today is guessing. That includes us, which is why the next section is labelled the way it is.

What Comparable Programs Paid, Which Is Not a Prediction

Since USDA says this is modelled on Farmer Bridge Assistance, what FBA paid is the closest thing to an anchor available. For the 2025 crop year, FBA paid corn $44.36 per acre, after applying a 30.41 percent factor to the calculated rate. Before that, ECAP paid corn $42.91 per acre for the 2024 crop year, factored at 85 percent.

Those are what happened, not what will happen. The pool, the factor and the crop year are all different here. Do not budget on them.

The Part That Favours Colorado, If It Holds

Here is the unusual thing. Both comparable programs paid a flat rate per acre by commodity, nationally. Under that structure a Colorado corn acre draws the same payment as an Iowa corn acre.

That is the opposite of how ARC and PLC treat this state. Those run off program yields, and Colorado’s sit below the national average because so much of our corn is dryland — which is why we keep warning members that national per-acre averages overstate what our base will pay. A flat acreage payment carries none of that penalty. Our acres count the same as anyone’s.

We are flagging it as a possibility, not a promise. “Modelled losses” is doing a lot of work in the announcement language, and a loss model built on national cost of production could be assembled in ways that do not produce a flat acreage rate. November will tell us. If it does hold, this is one of the better-shaped programs to come through for Colorado dryland in some time.

This Is Not the Diesel Order

These two keep getting run together, and they are different things.

The executive order signed October 5 lets off-road dyed diesel run on the highway and defers the 24.4 cent federal excise tax through the end of the year. Deferred is not forgiven. Five thousand on-road gallons is roughly $1,220 carried as a balance, not saved, and Colorado’s own 20.5 cent special fuel tax is a separate question an executive order does not reach.

Harvest Fuel Relief is a payment. It is money arriving rather than an obligation postponed. Both are responses to the same diesel problem — AAA has the national average around $6.32 a gallon — but only one of them puts a deposit in the account.

What to Do Today

Create a Login.gov account and link it to USDA. That is the whole list. It is free, it takes a few minutes, and USDA has said it is what separates a pre-filled application from a blank one when the window opens. Doing it now costs you nothing if the program changes shape.

Then watch for the November rate announcement. We will post the corn rate here when it lands, along with the application window, and we will say plainly if it comes in below what the comparables suggest.

If you grow something outside Title I, or you fish, USDA is asking for data on increased fuel costs at HarvestFuel@usda.gov so it can be considered in setting rates. That is an unusually open door and it will not stay open indefinitely.
