Farm Program Guides & Updates

A CCGA member program

Federal farm programs are not hard because the rules are complicated. They are hard because the rules change, the deadlines move, and nobody sends you a letter when a decision worth real money is about to close. This program exists to make sure a Colorado producer never misses one for lack of knowing.

What You Get

  • A written guide for every program that pays on Colorado corn — ARC and PLC, disaster programs, crop insurance interactions — in plain language, with the Colorado numbers rather than the national averages.
  • Deadline alerts before they matter, not the week they close. Email, with the date, what it turns on, and what happens if you do nothing.
  • Updates when a rule actually changes, with what changed and whether it changes anything for you.
  • Direct links to the tools — the decision aids, the calculators, the FSA and RMA pages that hold the authoritative numbers — so you can work your own figures instead of taking anyone's word.
  • A phone number. If something in a packet does not make sense for your operation, call and ask.

Why It Is Worth Reading

These are the kinds of things the packets exist to catch. Every one of them is real, and every one of them has cost somebody money.

Enrollment is required even if you change nothing. The 2026 ARC/PLC election and enrollment window closes December 11. Producers reasonably assume that leaving their election alone means leaving it alone. It does not. An operation that does not enroll is not enrolled, whatever it elected last year.

Your PLC yield is not your yield. It is a program yield on file at FSA, and it may have been set years ago on ground that has since been improved, irrigated or re-mapped. Producers estimate their payment off the yield they actually cut and get a different number than they expected.

Eligibility moves. Disaster eligibility that once reached 24 Colorado counties now reaches the whole state, because an argument was made and won. A producer who remembers the old map and assumes they are outside it does not file.

The safety net changed underneath everyone. H.R. 1 took the effective reference price for corn to $4.10 from $3.70, moved the ARC-County guarantee from 86 to 90 percent, raised payment limits from $125,000 to $155,000, and added up to 30 million acres of base nationally. Those are not small adjustments, and they are not automatic in anybody's planning.

None of this is secret. All of it is public, scattered across agency pages, handbooks and rule notices written for administrators rather than for the person driving the combine. What you are paying for is somebody reading all of it, on Colorado ground, and telling you the part that applies to you before the date passes.

What It Costs

$500 per operation, per year.

We are not going to tell you what you will get back, because nobody honestly can — it depends on your base acres, your yields, your election and the weather. What we will say is narrower and more useful: a single missed enrollment deadline costs more than the packet. That is the whole arithmetic.

The Checkoff Option

There is a second way to cover it, and it is worth understanding whether or not you use it.

Colorado corn producers pay a checkoff of one cent per bushel, collected at the point of sale. That is a refundable checkoff — a producer may request their own dollars back. An operation marketing 50,000 bushels pays $500 in checkoff. An operation marketing more pays more.

If you would rather direct that dollar toward this kind of work, you can request your refund and apply it to a packet. You would receive the guides and updates at no additional cost beyond the checkoff you already paid.

We want you to make that choice with both halves in front of you. A refund is not free money. Checkoff dollars fund research, market development and education, and a refund reduces that budget by exactly what you take back — including the share that benefits your own operation. What the checkoff legally cannot do is lobby; by statute, not one cent of it can be spent on policy work. We have written about that distinction at length, and it is the honest reason this association exists separately.

Some producers will read that and keep every checkoff dollar where it is. That is a completely reasonable decision and we are not going to argue with it. This is an option for producers who want to be more involved in where their own checkoff dollars go — not a campaign, and not a complaint about the checkoff.

What This Is Not

Read this part properly. It is short and it matters.

  • CCGA administers no USDA program and cannot approve, deny, expedite or appeal anything on your behalf.
  • These guides are general information, not advice — not legal advice, not tax advice, not financial advice, and not a substitute for your FSA county office, your accountant or your attorney.
  • Every figure should be confirmed against the agency before you act on it. Rules change mid-season and we will get something wrong eventually; when we do, we will say so and correct it in writing.
  • Decisions about your operation are yours. We can tell you a deadline exists. We cannot file for you and we do not know your books.

Enrollment requires agreeing to the program terms, which set this out in full.
