Should CCGA file an amicus brief in the Dryland Partners case?

Xcel's utility subsidiary lost a condemnation fight against a Cheyenne County farm on four independent grounds. It has now asked the Colorado Supreme Court to step in early. A grower association has one argument in this case that no party can make for itself — and one reason to think hard before making it.

Section 01

The short version

Read this firstEverything below supports or qualifies this page.

A district judge threw out Xcel's attempt to condemn roughly 550 feet of easement across a working Cheyenne County farm, finding the utility had no statutory authority, no public use, no necessity, and had negotiated in bad faith. Xcel has asked the Colorado Supreme Court to take the case immediately rather than wait for the Court of Appeals. The Colorado Energy Office and Tri-State are weighing briefs on Xcel's side. Nobody has yet filed on the landowner's side.

The board is being asked by Brian Dickey of Tenfold Farms to have CCGA weigh in. Before the board can vote, three things have to be settled: which filing we would be joining, who signs it, and when it is due. None of the three is currently known, because Xcel's petition has not reached us.

What is knowable today is whether CCGA has something worth saying. It does. Buried in paragraph 25 of the order is a finding that no party to this case will argue in the terms that matter to Colorado growers: condemnation at $19,886 would reset the valuation for every future easement negotiation at that substation. That is an agricultural-economics argument, not a utility-law argument, and it belongs to a growers' association.

There is also a real argument against involvement, and it is not the obvious one. It is set out in Section 08.

Section 04

What the court actually held

Four groundsEach independently sufficient. Xcel must beat all four.

The order is 16 pages and reaches four separate holdings. They are not equally durable on appeal, and the board should understand which is which before deciding how loudly to associate CCGA with the ruling.

NovelNo statutory authority to condemn

The court read C.R.S. § 38-4-103 — the electric power line right-of-way statute — as limited to mines and mining claims, reasoning that eleven of the sixteen statutes in Article 4 of Title 38 concern mines, so "or other land" cannot be read to swallow the mining language. § 38-4-107 was found to address compensation only. §§ 38-5-104 and 38-5-105 reach transmission and distribution to the public, not generation.

StrongA separate statute bars it outright

C.R.S. § 40-2-125, adopted by voters in 2004, says a qualifying retail utility may not condemn to site the generation facilities of a renewable energy system used to meet the electric resource standards. The court refused PSCo's reading that the bar applies only to petitions brought under § 38-2-101, holding that reading would let a utility escape a clear prohibition by changing what it calls the line.

If this type of gamesmanship was statutorily authorized, § 40-2-125 would be rendered meaningless, because a utility seeking to condemn private property for generating facilities could simply call them transmission facilities.Order, ¶ 15
ContestableNo public use

Applying the Tanner factors, the court found no benefit conferred on Cheyenne County — the power moves to the Front Range — and distinguished Public Service Co. v. Shaklee, where the line at issue served Coors but was also available to the public. Here the gen-ties are, under PSCo's own generator interconnection agreements, "sole use facilities" owned and operated by PSCo alone.

StrongNot necessary, and negotiated in bad faith

Dickey testified credibly that he spent "thousands of hours" building corridors PSCo initially approved in Section 24, before PSCo withdrew and moved on Sections 18 and 19 — ground PSCo knew was reserved as an oil and gas easement. The offer was $19,886.36 against a NextEra comparable the court described as worth millions. The court concluded PSCo selected the route that maximized harm while a viable, less damaging alternative existed.

The finding that anchors everything

Xcel's senior manager of siting and land rights testified that generation tie lines are "just a more specific name for a transmission line." The court found that testimony incredible — flatly contradicted by PSCo's own conditional use permit application, its cover letter naming the "Singing Grass Wind Generation Tie line," its 2022 RFP definition, and its FERC generator interconnection agreements.

This matters procedurally more than legally. Appellate courts do not re-weigh credibility findings. Xcel's petition has to persuade the Supreme Court that a pure question of law is cleanly presented, when three of the four grounds below rest on findings about what this utility did on this ground.

Section 05

Where the ruling is exposed

Be candidA board that only hears the good case makes a bad decision.

Two of the four holdings are genuinely vulnerable, and a third argument Dryland raised was rejected outright. If CCGA signs a brief, it should not tie itself to the weak parts.

The mining limitation is a novel reading

No Colorado appellate court has held that § 38-4-103 only reaches mines and mining claims. The statute has been on the books since 1907 and utilities have long assumed broader reach. This is the single most cert-worthy issue in the order and the one most likely to be reversed. It is also, unhelpfully, the ground the court reached first.

The public use analysis strains against Carousel Farms

The court wrote that generating electricity "only serves to benefit PSCo's profit margins." But Carousel Farms Metropolitan District v. Woodcrest Homes holds that private parties may benefit significantly, even substantially, so long as the purpose and benefit are essentially public. The court itself conceded that the fourth Tanner factor — necessity of the improvement to developing the state's resources — favors PSCo. A reviewing court could find the balance was struck too aggressively.

The permit-feasibility argument already lost

Dryland argued the project was infeasible because Cheyenne County would withhold permits after PSCo set pedestals in violation of the county's 75-foot setback. The court rejected it, citing Shaklee for the proposition that Colorado law does not require a condemning authority to obtain development permits as a condition precedent. Any CCGA brief should stay away from this line entirely.

The politics run the other direction

The Colorado Energy Office — a state agency — is lining up behind Xcel, as is Tri-State. Xcel is framing this as more than 1,000 megawatts of finished wind stranded behind 550 feet of ground. That framing has real force with a court weighing whether to intervene early, and CCGA should expect it to be the headline rather than the easement valuation question.

Section 06

Procedure, and why it decides everything

C.A.R. 29Governs amicus briefs. Leave of court is required. Word limits are hard.

"Support the landowners" is a posture, not a filing. There are two entirely different documents on the table, and they argue opposite things.

A petition-stage brief argues the court should stay out

Xcel is asking the Supreme Court to take the case immediately — the standard is that a case be of such imperative public importance as to justify deviation from normal appellate practice. An amicus supporting Dryland at this stage argues the opposite: that this is a fact-bound dispute about one utility's conduct on one route, that a Court of Appeals track already exists, and that the record does not present the clean legal question Xcel claims. Short, procedural, roughly 3,150 words.

A merits brief only exists if the court takes the case

Broader, later, and a different drafting problem. It would defend the statutory holdings — including the weak one.

The mechanics that constrain us

  • Leave of court is required. An amicus brief is filed with a motion for leave under C.A.R. 29(b). It is not filed as of right.
  • Word limits are firm. Roughly 3,150 words at the certiorari stage absent the court's permission, and the brief may be stricken for non-compliance.
  • The clock runs from the party's filing, not from ours. C.A.R. 29 keys the deadline to the filing being supported. That window may already be running.
  • A Colorado-licensed attorney must sign it. CCGA's board includes attorneys. One of them has to be counsel of record.
  • Disclosure is required. The brief must state whether a party's counsel authored it and whether anyone other than the amicus funded it.
  • The board must authorize the appearance. CCGA cannot appear in a Colorado Supreme Court proceeding on a phone call.

Confirm with Bradley Haight, Dryland's counsel, which filing is in play and what the deadline is, before the board votes. Everything else is premature until that answer comes back.

Section 07

The legislative track already failed

HB26-1278Postponed indefinitely 7–4, March 5, 2026.

This is the piece of the picture most directly in CCGA's lane, and it is not in the news coverage Brian sent.

Representative Chris Richardson of Elizabeth, with Senators Rod Pelton of Cheyenne Wells and Marc Snyder of Colorado Springs, ran HB26-1278 — a bill requiring an investor-owned utility to obtain a certificate of public convenience and necessity from the PUC and all local land use approvals before starting any condemnation proceeding for high-voltage transmission. It drew seventeen additional sponsors across both parties and both chambers.

The House Energy & Environment Committee adopted four amendments, then refused to refer it, 4–7, and postponed it indefinitely on the reverse roll call, 7–4, on March 5, 2026. The companion notice bill, HB26-1279, died the same way a week later.

Two things follow. First, the court is currently the only functioning venue — the legislature declined to act while these same disputes were live. Second, Senator Pelton represents Cheyenne Wells. A 2027 bill is a live prospect, and CCGA's Friday advocacy sessions are the right instrument for it. Whatever the board decides about the brief, the legislative file should be opened now.

Section 08

The case for and against signing

Both columnsare argued in good faith. The board decides.
For

Reasons to file

We have the one argument nobody else can make

Paragraph 25 of the order found that condemnation "would also change the valuation for all future private developers who want to connect to the Goose Creek Substation," and that at under $20,000 Dryland could no longer secure agreements worth millions. Dryland's counsel argues Dryland's case. Only a growers' association can tell the court what a sub-$20,000 condemnation comparable does to every Colorado producer sitting across the table from a wind or solar developer. That is the amicus function exactly: facts the parties cannot supply.

The field is currently one-sided

A state agency and Tri-State are weighing briefs for Xcel. Nothing has been filed for the landowner. A court reading only one set of amici gets one picture of who is affected.

The mechanism generalizes past this farm

The court found PSCo relabeled gen-ties as transmission lines for the first time in litigation. If that works, any developer can convert a negotiated easement market into a condemnation at will. Eastern Colorado ground is where the next decade of interconnection gets built.

It is precisely the advocacy we tell members we do

An independent, volunteer board of practicing farmers, attorneys and political veterans intervening in a Colorado Supreme Court proceeding on behalf of an Eastern Plains family operation is CCGA's stated purpose in its clearest possible form.

The legislature is closed to us right now

HB26-1278 died in committee. There is no bill to lobby this session. The record built in this case is what a 2027 bill would be built on.

Against

Reasons to decline

Our members are not on one side of this

More than a hundred landowners signed easements for these same two tie-lines. Many are growers. Some are glad to have the income. CCGA filing in support of the one holdout implies a membership position that the membership has not taken, and members with signed Xcel easements may reasonably read a brief as arguing their deals were struck under a broken system. That is a governance problem, not a legal one, and it does not go away by drafting carefully.

We would be attaching our name to a shaky holding

The mining limitation on § 38-4-103 is novel and may not survive. If the Supreme Court reverses, CCGA is publicly on the losing side of a property rights case — the opposite of the outcome we want on the record when the 2027 bill is drafted.

The valuation argument helps a specific landowner more than it helps growers generally

Paragraph 25 is about preserving Dryland's ability to command millions for an easement. A brief that leans hard on it can be read as an association arguing for higher easement prices rather than for property rights. Xcel's counsel will make exactly that read, and it lands differently with a court than it does at a county meeting.

Real cost, volunteer capacity

A compliant Supreme Court brief means counsel of record, a motion for leave, certification, and a filing deadline we do not yet control. This is a volunteer board in the middle of harvest.

There are lighter instruments

A board resolution, a letter of support to Dryland, testimony when a 2027 bill is heard, and coverage on our own channels all register the position without appearing as a party-adjacent filer in a case whose facts we did not develop.

Section 09

Recommendation

Not legal adviceThis memo was prepared for board discussion. Counsel must review before anything is filed.

Proposed motion

Authorize participation in principle, conditioned on four things, and open the legislative file regardless.

The strongest reason to file is real and specific: a growers' association can tell the court something about easement markets that no party in this case is positioned to say. The strongest reason to decline is also real: our membership is not uniformly on Dryland's side of this, and a brief speaks for all of them.

Those are reconcilable if the brief is narrow. Argue that this record does not present a clean statewide legal question and that a condemnation comparable set below market distorts easement negotiations for growers across the Eastern Plains. Do not defend the mining limitation. Do not touch the permit-feasibility argument. Do not argue Dryland's valuation.

  1. Counsel of record. A Colorado-licensed board attorney agrees to sign and take responsibility for compliance.
  2. Posture and deadline confirmed with Bradley Haight before drafting begins.
  3. Draft returns to the board for approval before filing. No one signs on the board's behalf sight unseen.
  4. Scope held to the two arguments above. If counsel concludes the brief cannot be kept that narrow, CCGA declines and issues a board resolution instead.

Separately and unconditionally: open the 2027 legislative file on HB26-1278. Senator Pelton represents Cheyenne Wells and carried it. That work is squarely ours and carries none of the membership risk the brief does.
